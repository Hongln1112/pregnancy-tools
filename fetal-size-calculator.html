How to use this calculator

  • Enter how many weeks pregnant you are to see your baby's fruit size, length, and average weight for that week.
  • If you have an ultrasound weight estimate, add it in grams to see whether your baby is below, around, or above the weekly average.
  • Growth is steady and individual — check back each week and talk to your care team about any concerns.

About the estimates

Sizes and weights are general estimates for illustration

Every pregnancy grows a little differently, and ultrasound measurements are the most accurate way to check your baby's size and weight. The averages here are typical references — from around 300 g (10 oz) at week 20 to roughly 3,400 g (7.5 lb) at week 40 — but your baby's actual scan numbers are what matter most.

Common questions

How big is my baby right now?▾
Our fetal size estimates are based on average measurements from standard growth charts. At each week, we show the typical weight and length of the baby. Remember — these are averages. Your baby might be a little bigger or smaller, and that's completely normal. Every baby grows at their own pace.
Why does everyone compare babies to fruit?▾
It's a fun, easy way to visualize how big your baby is. At 8 weeks, your baby is the size of a kidney bean. At 16 weeks, an avocado. At 20 weeks, a banana. By 40 weeks, a small watermelon. It helps make the numbers feel more real and relatable for expectant parents.
Is my baby measuring too small or too big?▾
Only your doctor can tell you that. Your ultrasound will measure the baby's head circumference, belly circumference, and femur length, and compare them to growth charts. If your baby is measuring in the 10th–90th percentile, that's normal. If they're outside that range, your provider will talk to you about next steps.
When will my ultrasound measurements match my due date?▾
Early ultrasounds (8–12 weeks) are very accurate for dating. Later in pregnancy, babies grow at different rates, so ultrasounds are less precise for dating — they're more for checking growth. That's why your due date is usually set from your first trimester ultrasound, not later ones.
